Notes

A note is one thing that broke and the rule left over from it. Deliberately short: if it needs more than three hundred words, it becomes a case study.

Opened in August 2026 with five notes at once, which already existed buried inside the case studies. The three from November are about things that happened later: a migration, a button that lied and a deadline that did not fit inside the deadline. Each one cites its source.

06.11.2026 Kyber CRM Infrastructure

After the first write, there is no going back

On 31 October the CRM moved off Vercel and Supabase onto its own server. The obvious rollback plan, point DNS back if something goes wrong, only works until the destination takes its first write. After that, going back to the origin silently erases everything done on the destination.

So the origin was locked before the final capture, the restore ran in a single transaction and only committed after comparing 146 tables, 11 sequences and the files by hash. And the rule was written down before the cutover: once open, the destination is recovered, never swapped out.

A migration rollback has an expiry date, and it expires at the first write. Decide before the cutover what happens after it.

06.11.2026 Kyber CRM Product

Opening WhatsApp is not sending

The "Send on WhatsApp" button opened the chat and, in the same click, marked the proposal as sent and logged the contact. But opening is not sending: the person may close the window, change the text or give up. The system was asserting a fact nobody had confirmed, and the whole funnel counted on top of it.

Now there are two gestures. "Open in WhatsApp" only opens. "I sent it" records it and returns an explicit outcome: recorded, already recorded, partial or refused with a reason. Clicking twice duplicates nothing, with no migration: the state change is an UPDATE conditional on draft status, and the contact has an id derived from the proposal.

A click records what the click does, not what is expected to follow. What follows needs its own button.

06.11.2026 Didata Reliability

The deadline has to fit inside the deadline

Two activity generations failed in the same week and the log only said "timeout, cleanup". It was the cleanup job closing, half an hour later, whatever had been left pending. The original cause was nowhere, because the function died at the platform's 60-second limit before it could record why it died.

Generation now has an absolute 55-second deadline from entry. When it expires it aborts the provider, refuses late responses and uses the 5 seconds left to record the cause, one of six typed ones, and the duration. A retry only starts if there are 10 useful seconds, and never after a partial result has been delivered.

If the outer limit kills the process, the inner one has to expire first, with room left to say what happened. A failure with no recorded cause is a failure that will happen again.

05.08.2026 Didata Infrastructure

The pooler that hangs instead of failing

Seven deploys in a row died at the 45-minute timeout, with no error message. The cause was the Postgres transaction pooler, which is the right connection for serverless runtime and the wrong one for migrations: it does not support migrate deploy's long DDL transactions, and the command hangs instead of refusing.

The runtime URL and the CLI URL are two variables, not one. And the same variable has to exist on the platform and in CI secrets, because CI runs the same build.

05.08.2026 Didata Architecture

A 1 MB limit that became an architecture rule

Raising the Server Action body limit broke the build with a type error, and only on the platform's own infrastructure: the local build did not reproduce it. It took about six hours of bisecting to isolate, and the outcome was not being able to raise it.

So the limit stayed at the 1 MB default, no Server Action can take a file, and every upload in the product is a route handler. A whole architecture rule was born from someone else's bug that was never fixed.

A workaround is not a design. It is worth writing down which of the two it is, because whoever arrives later cannot tell, and one day the bug goes away and the rule is left with no reason.

05.08.2026 Kyber CRM, Didata Security

RLS in the database or authorisation in the application

Two of my products, both on Postgres, with opposite choices. The CRM has 276 live row-level-security policies over 78 tables: the database refuses the row, whatever happens in the application. Didata authorises in the application.

The difference is not maturity, it is how many doors exist. The CRM is reached by a dashboard, by a 121-tool MCP server, by token-based public pages, by scheduled jobs and, since October, by a content studio. Five doors, and the rule written at each one would be the same rule written five times, drifting apart at the first rush. Didata has one door.

Count the doors before choosing where the rule lives. One door, the application is enough and costs less. More than one, the rule goes down into the database, otherwise it becomes several rules.

05.08.2026 Didata Product

Why the AI suggests and does not confirm

The model returns one alternative per question plus a confidence score from 0 to 100. Writing the grade straight through above a threshold would be trivial, and the system does not: suggested and confirmed are distinct states in the database, and the report card only counts what the teacher confirmed. Including the high-confidence ones.

It costs a lot in convenience: an exam of 30 students by 10 questions is 300 readings to accept. The mitigation was not to loosen the rule, it was to make accepting cheaper, with batch confirmation and a queue that surfaces what needs attention. The queue's bias is declared and errs toward caution: a question with no reading at all counts as uncertain. In October, the national education council's opinion CNE/CP 15/2026 removed batch confirmation: it is now question by question, and on written answers the AI only transcribes. The queue stayed.

When the rule hurts, the way out is to lower the cost of obeying it, not to open an exception. The first exception is the one nobody can explain six months later.

05.08.2026 Brava Integration

The infinite wait is the most expensive symptom

On the day of connecting the panel to production, requests hung forever. The server's RPC framework has two independent transports, and the resource I was calling had only registered one of them. The event went out and nobody was listening. No error, no timeout, no log.

The fix was one line. The diagnosis was not, because a silent failure does not tell you where to look, and I had tested each side on its own: both passed.

A seam bug does not show up when you test the ends. Before migrating thirteen screens, one smoke test crossing the whole stack, from HTTP down to the database, just once.
